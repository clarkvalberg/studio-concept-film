# Philosophy

This is the long version of the README's "Why it exists" section. Read it for scope, design principles, and the judgment behind the workflow.

## What a concept film is for

A concept film is the moment a concept becomes legible. Not legible to the team that built it — the team can hold the concept in their heads from a Notion doc and a Figma file. Legible to someone outside the team: an LP, a prospective hire, a customer in the first conversation, a partner who needs to make a decision in eight minutes.

Many ideas become hard to evaluate because they are not yet legible to anyone outside the room they were born in. The concept film exists to close that gap. It is a thinking artifact, not just a marketing artifact. The difference matters.

A marketing artifact is built to persuade. A concept film is built to clarify. Persuasion is easier when the clarity is already there.

<br />

## Scope

**It is not a general video tool.** You bring source material. The skill turns it into one specific format: 60–90 seconds, voiceover-driven, prototype-grounded, insight-led. If you want a tutorial, an ad, a recorded demo, a social post, or a five-minute explainer, another format is a better fit.

**It uses curated choices.** The skill picks. You confirm or correct. There are three structural variants, not twelve. There are eight default voices, not eighty. There is one canonical structure with named variants, not a builder.

**It behaves like a workflow.** The skill gates. It decides silently where it can. It pauses at legibility checks. It tells you which phase you are in. It does not ask three questions when one would do.

**It is not a "magic" black box.** Every artifact it produces is editable. The script lives in a Markdown file you can edit. The design tokens live in JSON you can tune. The HyperFrames project is yours to refine. The skill is the starting line, not the finish.

## The form's defining qualities

The form this skill produces has five working principles. They are based on patterns common to effective product concept films.

### 1. The product is the protagonist's tool, not the protagonist's identity

The film does not say "people who use [product] are different kinds of people." It says "here is a person doing their work, and the product is in their hand." The product earns its place by being useful, not by being aspirational signaling.

The stronger films show people doing real work, with the product changing what happens next.

### 2. The voiceover speaks **to** the viewer, not **at** them

The voice is warm-confident. Adult. Slightly aspirational without ever being breathless. It treats the viewer as an intelligent peer who can fill in gaps on their own.

The voice is **not**:
- The "epic" register — sweeping music + grand claims. Reads as parody now.
- The "best friend" register — performatively casual. Reads as a podcast ad.
- The "demo" register — feature-listing, present-tense, breathless. Reads as a webinar.

### 3. Real screens, not abstract metaphors

The product is shown. Viewers should leave knowing what it looks like, what it does, and what the core ontology is. Abstract metaphors can make the concept feel less concrete.

Schematic stand-ins are acceptable when real screens don't exist — but the film flags it as a constraint, not a feature.

### 4. The music builds; the film lands

The film has rhythm. The cold open is short. The problem builds. The insight is a *moment* — often the shortest section. The product walk is the longest. The vision close lands hard and gets out.

Pacing is not even. Even pacing reads as flat. The film must breathe, then move, then breathe again.

### 5. Aspiration is earned through specificity

The vision close lands on something specific the product enables, not something abstract it represents. "Software for people who have things to do" can only describe one product. "Reimagining the future of work" describes none.

The film earns its closing line through every specific that came before. If you cut the closing line and pasted it into a competitor's film, it should not fit.

## The principle of recognition over persuasion

A concept film succeeds when the viewer experiences recognition: the moment they think *"yes, that's the thing I've been trying to name."*

Recognition requires that the film accurately *names* something the viewer was already half-thinking, then presents it back with more clarity.

Everything in this skill — the legibility gate, the banned words, the insight section, the warm-confident voice — supports that recognition.

Persuasion is downstream. If recognition does not land, more persuasion usually does not fix the film.

<br />

## Why the banned words list

Words become banned not because they lack meaning but because they have been worn through. "Revolutionary" once meant something. "Game-changing" once described actual category shifts. "Leverage" once described actual asymmetric advantage. These words are now heavily overused in pitch decks, product pages, and earnings calls.

Using them can make a script feel less specific. The viewer may hear the category language before they hear the idea.

The banned words list is a simple quality guardrail. It keeps the script closer to concrete nouns, specific verbs, and observable product behavior.

## On workflow

The structures, timing budgets, variant taxonomy, and voice shortlist are workflow defaults. They exist to keep the script, design, audio, and render loop moving in the same direction.

The hard part is making the structure, script, visuals, pacing, and voice work together. The workflow compresses those decisions into a sequence of reviewable gates.

The skill is not a substitute for judgment. It is a way to make the judgment moments explicit.

<br />

## A final note on tools and judgment

A skill that picks for you is not a skill that thinks for you. The skill picks the variant, but you confirm. The skill writes the script, but you edit. The skill chooses the four voices to audition, but you select. The skill renders the hook, but you decide if it lands.

The judgment remains yours. The skill compresses the time between judgment moments, removes the friction of starting from blank, and applies guardrails before the film becomes generic.

That's the point.
