# Script Writing Rules

The script is the spine of the film. Everything — voice selection, pacing, visual rhythm, render timing — flows from the script. Get this right and the rest aligns; get it wrong and no design polish recovers it.

## Hard constraints

**Word count.** Standard voiceover pace is ~150 words per minute. Concept films benefit from *slower* than standard — closer to 130 wpm — because the silence carries weight.

| Film length | Word budget | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 60s | 130–150 words | Tight. Every word fights for its slot. |
| 75s | 165–190 words | The most forgiving length. |
| 90s | 195–220 words | Maximum. Beyond this, you're padding. |
| 120s | 250–280 words | Only if the concept truly needs it. |

Never write past the upper bound for your chosen length. If the draft exceeds it, cut — don't extend the film.

## Voice and tone

**The voice of a concept film:**
- Warm, but not folksy
- Confident, but not arrogant
- Specific, never sweeping
- Curious, not promotional
- Adult — the viewer is treated as an intelligent peer

**Three useful tonal anchors to think about:**
- The way an Apple product introduction is *narrated* (not the keynote stage voice, the film voice)
- The way Stripe writes its homepage copy
- The way a New York Times documentary short does voiceover

**What to avoid:**
- The "epic" register — sweeping music + grand claims. Reads as a parody now.
- The "best friend" register — overly casual, performative warmth. Reads as a podcast ad.
- The "demo" register — feature-listing, present-tense, breathless. Reads as a webinar.

## Sentence-level craft

**Short sentences. Then longer ones. Then very short.**

Rhythm matters more than information density. A concept film is *heard*, not read. The ear forgives much, but it does not forgive monotony.

**Open with a concrete image, not a category.**

Bad: "In the modern enterprise, productivity is increasingly fragmented."
Good: "Sarah opens her laptop. Twelve tabs. Three unread Slacks. The day hasn't started."

**Prefer verbs over adjectives. Prefer nouns over adverbs.**

Bad: "Powerful, intelligent automation that seamlessly handles complex workflows."
Good: "It reads the document. Catches the gaps. Flags the deadlines."

**Use the second person sparingly.**

"You" works in the vision close. It often fails earlier — it presumes a relationship the film hasn't earned yet. Default to third person or to the product as subject.

**Name the product at least once. Twice is better. Three times starts to feel like an ad.**

Place the product name at the moment it would appear in real use — typically at the transition from insight to product walk, then again in the vision close.

**End on a single, plain sentence.**

The film's last line should be short, declarative, unornamented. Often just the product name and a tagline. Resist the urge to summarize.

## Banned words and phrases

These are banned not because they're meaningless but because they're *exhausted*. They no longer carry signal. Using them marks the film as generic before the viewer can even hear the idea.

**Banned outright:**
- revolutionary
- game-changing / game-changer
- best-in-class / world-class
- cutting-edge
- next-generation / next-gen
- leverage (as verb)
- unlock (when used metaphorically)
- empower
- seamlessly
- innovative / innovation (in self-description; OK in problem framing)
- transformative (in self-description)
- synergy
- holistic
- robust
- scalable (in pitch context — fine in technical context)
- mission-critical
- enterprise-grade
- supercharge / turbocharge
- AI-powered (use specific verb instead)

**Almost always banned:**
- "designed to" → just say what it does
- "helps you to" → just say what happens
- "imagine a world where" → tired opener
- "we believe" → fine in vision close, weak anywhere else

**Use sparingly:**
- "platform" — usually a duck-out from naming what the product actually is
- "solution" — same
- "experience" — when in doubt, replace with the specific noun

## Visual instructions in the script

Every section of the script has both VO (voiceover) and an on-screen direction. The on-screen direction is for the Remotion template — keep it concrete enough that a scene can be built from it.

**Format:**

```markdown
## Cold Open (0:00–0:08)

**[On-screen:** A hand placing a stack of paper files onto a desk. Close-up. Warm tungsten light. The stack is taller than expected.**]**

**VO:** It's the 14th of the month. Russ has 312 compliance documents on his desk.
```

The on-screen direction should suggest:
- Subject (what's in frame)
- Composition (close-up, wide, medium)
- Lighting / mood
- Movement (still, slow push, cut)
- Any kinetic typography or UI overlay

You don't have to write cinematography poetry. Just enough that the Remotion scene can be filled with intent, not guesswork.

## Section-by-section guidance

### Cold Open

- One image, one beat. Do not stack multiple ideas here.
- The VO should be a fragment or a short sentence. Not a thesis.
- If you can write the cold open without using a single adjective, you've probably nailed it.

### Problem / Context

- Make the friction physical. The viewer should feel something pinch.
- Avoid statistics unless they're surprising. "73% of businesses…" is anesthetic.
- One or two specifics > five generalities.

### Insight

- The shortest section by word count, often. The insight is a *moment* — give it air.
- Frame it as a turn: "But." "Except." "What if." "Here's what's actually true."
- The insight should be true even if your product didn't exist. It's structural.

### Product Walk

- Pick the 2–3 capabilities that ladder to the insight. Not 7. Not "everything."
- Each capability gets one short sentence and one screen.
- Show the product working, not the product's marketing.
- This is where the product name appears for the first time, almost always.

### Vision Close

- Land on something specific the product enables, not something abstract it represents.
- The brand mark appears here, late. Often the final 3 seconds.
- The closing line should be reproducible in conversation — if someone says "what's the film about" the closing line should be the answer.

## Common script failures and how to fix them

**Failure: "It feels like an ad."**
Cause: the script is selling, not framing. Fix: cut every sentence whose function is to persuade. Keep only sentences that observe, describe, or explain. The persuasion happens in the viewer's head when the observations are sharp enough.

**Failure: "It's not landing emotionally."**
Cause: too abstract too early. Fix: replace the most abstract sentence in the first 30 seconds with a concrete image — a person, a place, a moment, an object.

**Failure: "It doesn't feel like *this* product."**
Cause: the script could describe any product in the category. Fix: identify the one thing this concept does that no competitor does, and make sure that thing is in the insight or product walk explicitly.

**Failure: "The ending feels flat."**
Cause: the vision close is generic. Fix: replace the closing line with something specific enough that it could only describe this product. "Software for people who have things to do" works for one product. "Reimagining work" works for none.

**Failure: "It's too long."**
Cause: usually the problem section. Fix: cut the problem section by a third. Most viewers know the problem; you only need to ratify it, not prove it.

## A final principle

Concept films aren't won on persuasion. They're won on *recognition* — the moment the viewer thinks "yes, that's the thing I've been trying to name." Write toward recognition, not toward conviction. The film's job is to make the concept legible. Persuasion follows for free if legibility lands.
